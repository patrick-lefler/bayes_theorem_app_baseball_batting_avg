1. The Mystery: The April .400 Hitter

Every April, a handful of Major League hitters go 4-for-10 in their first few games and boast a blistering .400 batting average. Does anyone actually believe they are a true .400 hitter who will break Ted Williams' record?

The Coin Toss Problem: If you flip a coin 10 times and get 7 heads (70%), you don't conclude the coin is broken. You know 10 flips is too small a sample to overcome your prior belief that coins are 50/50. Baseball batting works the exact same way.

Standard batting statistics (H / AB) suffer from small sample noise. Empirical Bayes solves this by anchoring noisy early-season samples to historical prior distributions.

2. The Beta Distribution as "Pseudo-At-Bats"

A batter's true underlying batting talent θ is modeled using a Beta distribution:

θ ~ Beta(α₀, β₀)

The parameters α₀ and β₀ have an intuitive interpretation in baseball:

  • α₀ (Alpha): Pseudo-hits prior to the season (e.g., 75).
  • β₀ (Beta): Pseudo-outs prior to the season (e.g., 225).
  • Total Prior Weight (α₀ + β₀): The equivalent sample size of historical data (e.g., 300 pseudo-at-bats).
  • Prior Mean: α₀ / (α₀ + β₀) = 75 / 300 = .250 (league average).

3. The Math: Beta-Binomial Conjugacy

Because the Beta distribution is the conjugate prior to the Binomial likelihood, updating your belief after observing new hits (H) and outs (AB - H) requires no complex integration—just simple arithmetic:

α₁ = α₀ + H and β₁ = β₀ + (AB - H)

The new Bayes expected batting average is the mean of this updated distribution:

E[θ | H, AB] = (α₀ + H) / (α₀ + β₀ + AB)

4. Empirical Shrinkage in Action

The Bayes estimate can also be written as a weighted average between the player's noisy in-season average and the prior:

Bayes BA = (1 - w) × Prior Mean + w × Observed BA

where the weight on the new data is:

w = AB / (AB + α₀ + β₀)
  • After 10 At-Bats (4/10): w = 10 / (10 + 300) = 3.2%. The observed .400 average receives only 3.2% weight, shrinking the estimate back to .255!
  • After 500 At-Bats (160/500): w = 500 / (500 + 300) = 62.5%. The season data now dominates the prior, pulling the estimate close to the player's true .320 performance.
